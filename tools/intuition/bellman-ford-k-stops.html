Start with what you would do by hand

Four cities. Flights: 0 → 1 costs 100, 1 → 2 costs 100, 2 → 0 costs 100, 1 → 3 costs 600, 2 → 3 costs 200. You want the cheapest way from 0 to 3 with at most 1 stop, that is, at most 2 flights. By hand: 0 → 1 → 3 costs 700 and uses 2 flights. 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 costs 400 but uses 3 flights, too many. So the answer is 700.

Notice that the cheapest route overall is not allowed. Dijkstra would settle city 2 at 200 and then happily reach 3 for 400, ignoring the stop limit. We need a method where the number of flights is part of the bookkeeping.

Build the structure, one piece at a time

Piece one: a table dist[] of the cheapest cost found so far to each city, starting as {0: 0, 1: ∞, 2: ∞, 3: ∞}.

Piece two: the idea of a round. In one round, go through every flight once and relax it: if dist[u] + price < dist[v], set dist[v] to the smaller value. After one round, every city reachable with one flight has its correct 1-flight cost.

Piece three, the rule that enforces the limit: in each round, read from a copy of the previous round's table and write into a fresh one. Otherwise an update made early in the round could be reused later in the same round, letting a path grow by two flights in one round.

Run it. Round 1 (reads {0, ∞, ∞, ∞}): 0 → 1 gives 100; every other flight starts from an ∞ city and does nothing. Table after round 1: {0, 100, ∞, ∞}. Round 2 (reads that copy): 1 → 2 gives 200, 1 → 3 gives 700, 2 → 3 reads the copy's ∞ for city 2 and does nothing. Table after round 2: {0, 100, 200, 700}. With k = 1 stop we run k + 1 = 2 rounds and stop. dist[3] = 700.

Why it is correct

The invariant: after round i, dist[v] is the cheapest cost of any route to v that uses at most i flights. It holds for i = 0. If it holds after round i, then the cheapest route with at most i + 1 flights is either a route with at most i flights (already in the copy) or a route with at most i flights to some u followed by one flight u → v, and round i + 1 tries exactly that for every flight. Reading from the copy guarantees the "at most i flights to u" part was not padded during the current round.

This also handles negative prices, which Dijkstra cannot: nothing in the argument assumed a price is positive.

Why it is fast

Each round touches every flight once, and there are k + 1 rounds:

work = (k + 1) · E

For 100 cities, 5,000 flights and k = 3 that is 20,000 relaxations. Without the limit, the classic Bellman–Ford runs V − 1 rounds for the same reason: no shortest path uses more than V − 1 edges.

How to recognise it in an interview

Cues: "at most k stops", "within k edges", "negative weights", "cheapest route with a limit on legs". The first thing to say: "the limit is on edges, so I run k + 1 relaxation rounds from a copy of the previous table." Cheapest Flights Within K Stops is this exact routine. Network Delay Time has no limit, so Dijkstra is the better fit there; mention Bellman–Ford only if the interviewer adds negative times.
