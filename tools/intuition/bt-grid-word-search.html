Start with what you would do by hand

A 3×4 board of letters, and the word SEE. Does the word appear as a path of neighbouring cells, stepping up, down, left or right, never reusing a cell? By hand you look for an S, then look around it for an E, then around that E for another E that is not the one you just used. If a route dies you go back to the S and try another direction.

That is the whole algorithm, and the hard part is the bookkeeping: remembering which cells the current route has used so you do not step back onto them, and forgetting them correctly when you abandon the route. Done by hand with a pencil, you would mark cells and then rub the marks out.

Build the structure, one piece at a time

Piece one: the board itself, a grid you can read by row and column.

Piece two: a depth, how many letters of the word have matched so far along the current route.

Piece three: marks. Instead of a separate visited grid, overwrite the current cell with a placeholder such as # while exploring from it, and write the letter back when you return. The marked cells are exactly the current route, the pencil marks from the by-hand version.

Piece four, the rule, search(row, col, depth): if the cell is off the board, marked, or its letter is not word[depth], fail. If depth is the last letter, succeed. Otherwise mark the cell, try the four neighbours with depth + 1, unmark, and report whether any neighbour succeeded. Run search from every cell as a starting point.

Why it is correct

The invariant: the marked cells are exactly the cells on the current route. Marking happens when a cell joins the route and unmarking when the route backs off it, in stack order, so a mark never outlives its route. Because of the invariant, a cell is never used twice in one word, which is the problem's rule, and because marks are removed on return, a cell that failed on one route is free to be used by a later route that reaches it differently. Every route of length equal to the word is examined in the worst case, so a word that exists is found.

Why it is fast

From a start cell, the first step has up to 4 directions and every later step at most 3 (you cannot step back onto the cell you came from, it is marked). For a word of length L on an m×n board:

work ≤ m·n · 4 · 3L−1, usually written O(m·n · 4L)

The letter check at each step kills most branches immediately, which is why it runs fast in practice; the marking costs nothing extra because it reuses the board.

How to recognise it in an interview

Cues: "path in a grid", "adjacent cells", "each cell used at most once", "does the word exist", "visit every empty cell exactly once". The first thing to say: "DFS from each cell, mark on entry, unmark on return." Word Search is the example above. Word Search II has many words; build a trie of them and walk the trie alongside the board so one DFS finds all words at once. Unique Paths III is the same mark-and-unmark walk counting routes that cover every open cell.
