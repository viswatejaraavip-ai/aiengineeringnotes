Start with what you would do by hand

From −1, 0, 1, 2, −1, −4, find every set of three numbers that adds to 0, without listing the same set twice. The answers are (−1, −1, 2) and (−1, 0, 1). By hand you would try triples: six numbers make 20 triples, and for n numbers about n3 ÷ 6. A thousand numbers is 166 million triples, and you would still have to weed out duplicates like (−1, 0, 1) found twice from the two −1s.

Two-sum on a sorted list is cheap (two pointers, O(n)). The question is how to turn three-sum into many two-sums without paying too much.

Build the structure, one piece at a time

Piece one: sort. −4, −1, −1, 0, 1, 2. Sorting is what makes both the two-pointer walk and the duplicate skipping possible.

Piece two: pin the first number. Walk an index i from left to right; for each one, the remaining job is "find two numbers in positions after i that add to −nums[i]". That is exactly the sorted two-sum, so run the converging two pointers l = i + 1 and r = last on the suffix.

Piece three: skip duplicates at every level. If nums[i] equals nums[i − 1], every triple starting here was already found from the previous i, so skip. Likewise after recording a triple, step l past repeated values and r past repeated values.

You can stop early once nums[i] > 0: with a sorted list, three positives cannot sum to zero.

Why it is correct

Two invariants. The outer one: when the pin is at i, every triple whose smallest index is less than i has already been reported. The inner one is the two-sum invariant: the pair, if it exists in the suffix, lies within [l, r]. Together they mean every zero-sum triple is found when its smallest index is pinned.

Why no duplicates? A set of three values, in sorted order, is (a, b, c). It is reported only when the pin sits on the first occurrence of a (later equal pins are skipped) and the pointers sit on the first b and last c they meet; after reporting, both pointers jump past equal values, so the same (b, c) is not seen again for this pin. Each value-set is therefore reported exactly once.

Why it is fast

Sorting costs O(n log n). Each pin runs one two-pointer walk over at most n elements, O(n), and there are n pins:

n log n + n · n = O(n2)

For a thousand numbers that is about a million steps instead of 166 million triples. The pattern generalises: k-sum is k − 2 nested pins around one two-pointer walk, O(nk − 1).

How to recognise it in an interview

Cues: "three (or four) numbers that sum to", "closest to a target", "count triples with …", and the word "unique" or "no duplicate triples". Say: "Sort, fix the first element, and solve the rest as a two-pointer two-sum on the suffix, skipping duplicates at each level."

3Sum is the example. 3Sum Closest keeps the best |sum − target| seen during the walk instead of stopping at equality. Valid Triangle Number pins the largest side and counts how many pairs in the prefix satisfy a + b > c, where a "too small" sum lets you move l and a valid one lets you count r − l pairs at once.
